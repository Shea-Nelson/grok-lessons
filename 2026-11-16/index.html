Teammates: let your bots hand off work

Good morning, Shea. This week: teammates and group chats — how your bots pass work to each other cleanly, without duplicate effort or surprise messages.

This week’s lesson: teammates, one owner per job

You already run a crew: Scorpy for the day-to-day, Zhaan for family logistics, Rygel for shopping, Tim for the house and cars, Harvey for phone calls. The official help calls these your teammates. A Bot can send another Bot an asynchronous message to hand off work, and you can see that handoff in the conversation. For work that needs several Bots at once, a group chat gives them one shared outcome and visible handoffs.

A few rules from the official “Message and collaborate” page are worth knowing. Every Bot reply counts toward your usage, including replies in a group and messages between Bots. Parallel handoffs create duplicate work and noisy updates. Approval requests, sign-in requests, and email drafts don’t appear in a group; they show up in a Bot’s own chat. Handoff messages to a group are text-only, so a Bot should send an image straight to the Bot that needs to see it. There’s no switch to stop Bots messaging each other; they follow what you tell them.

  1. Give each job one owner

    Decide who owns what, in plain words: Zhaan owns the calendar, Rygel owns prices, Scorpy owns the keto meal plan. One owner per job is what keeps handoffs from doubling up.

  2. Hand off in a sentence

    In one Bot’s chat, ask it to pass a piece to the owner: what to do, what to send back, and where the result should land.

  3. Use a group only for shared outcomes

    A holiday plan that needs calendar dates, a shopping list, and a menu fits a group. A single question to one Bot doesn’t. In a group, type @ and pick the Bot that owns the request; save @everyone for real group-wide news.

  4. Keep replies where you want them

    Bots can answer you privately even when you asked in a group. If you want them in the group, say “Reply here in the group,” or have each Bot remember it as a rule.

  5. Keep sends behind your OK

    Anything for Nicole still goes the usual way: a draft for Giles that you approve. A handoff between your own Bots is not permission to email or text anyone.

Copy and try 
Hand this to Zhaan, one message, no group: check my iPhone calendar for Thanksgiving week and list every night we already have plans. Ask her to send the list back to you, not to me. Then use it to skip those nights in next week's keto meal plan, and give me only the updated Page link plus one line on what changed.

Tip of the week

Hand off one thing at a time, to one Bot. The official help says parallel handoffs create duplicate work, and every Bot-to-Bot message uses your weekly allowance. If a chain starts bouncing back and forth, tell the Bots in that chat: “Don’t message other Bots about this. Report to me.”

Use case spotlight: a school-test catcher

A parent on X shared a three-Bot chain that caught a test the school added on a Friday night. A school Bot checks the timetable every morning, read-only. When a new test shows up, a calendar Bot adds it after a duplicate check, and a chief-of-staff Bot sits at the end of the chain. It’s the same shape as one owner per job: each Bot has a narrow role and passes along only what’s new.

For your family, it could look like this: Zhaan watches the kids’ school and band emails you already forward, adds new dates after checking for duplicates, and gives Scorpy a heads-up only when a date changes a dinner night.
