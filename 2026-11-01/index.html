Routines: your bot on a clock (or on alert)

Good morning, Shea. This week: routines — put a job on a schedule, or start it when something happens, and keep anything that messages Nicole or posts to Discord behind your OK.

This week’s lesson: a routine says when, not how

A skill is how to do a job. A routine tells one Bot when to run it: on a schedule, or when something happens, such as a new email or a webhook call. Official help is clear on the order: start with a one-time task, make it reliable, save the method as a skill, and only then automate it.

Routines run in the cloud while your laptop is closed. Schedules use the time zone in Settings, under Bot, labeled Timezone. Ask in plain words, like “every weekday at 8:00 AM,” then open the routine and check When to run, which shows the saved schedule in words.

A new routine waits for its next scheduled time. It doesn’t run when you create it. If you set “every day at 8:00 AM” at 8:05, the first run is tomorrow at 8:00 AM. To try it right away, use Test, when the routine offers it.

  1. Pick one job and one owner

    A Sunday keto dinner plan, a Monday look at your iPhone calendar, or a weekly grocery list. Give that job to one Bot, the same way Zhaan owns calendar dates.

  2. Write the when, the result, and the stop line

    Say the schedule or the event, what should come back (a Page link, a short note in this chat), and what must wait for you: an email to Giles, a Discord post, or anything that spends money.

  3. Check When to run

    Open the routine and read the schedule in words. If it doesn’t match what you meant, choose Edit and tell the Bot what to fix.

  4. Test with a safe example

    A test run does real work. It can open websites and call connected tools. Use a safe input, and keep sends behind your OK.

  5. Watch how often it runs

    Each run spends usage. An hourly schedule, a short interval, or a busy-channel trigger can use a week of usage in a day. A routine that never runs spends nothing; Test does, because it performs the work.

Copy and try 
Set up a routine for me: every Sunday at 4:00 PM Eastern, draft next week's keto dinner plan from our usual rotation and my iPhone calendar, skip nights that already have plans, and update the existing Meal Plan Page in place. Reply in this chat with only the Page link and two lines on what changed. Do not email Giles, post to Discord, or buy anything. If the calendar can't be read, say so and stop; don't guess the week.

Tip of the week

A new routine does not run the moment you save it. If you set “every day at 8 AM” at 8:05, the first run is tomorrow at 8. Open the routine and hit Test to try it now — and use a safe example, because a test does real work. Keep anything that messages Nicole, posts to Discord, or spends money behind your OK. If your weekly limit is running down faster than you expect, pause routines you don’t need, then widen schedules and narrow event triggers.

Use case spotlight: an iPhone alarm that starts a briefing

On November 5, Michael (@Michael_Fenech_) wrote that he made Grok Bot respond to a real-world event with his iPhone. When he stops his morning alarm, it starts preparing his daily briefing. He tested it that day and said it worked: no fixed schedule, no opening Grok Bot, and no typing a prompt. In follow-up replies he said it triggers when the alarm is fully stopped, not on snooze.

For your mornings, a scheduled routine is still the simple start: a short look at the iPhone calendar, in this chat. An event trigger is useful when the clock is the wrong cue. Official help also describes webhook triggers, which other people in that thread discussed. His post doesn’t spell out a Shortcuts recipe, so I won’t invent one here.
