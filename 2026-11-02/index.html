Skills: teach it once and it sticks

Good morning, Shea. This week: skills — write down how a job should be done, so you don’t re-explain the keto plan, the grocery list, or the Giles draft rules every time.

This week’s lesson: a skill is the how

A skill is a reusable set of instructions for how to do a task. Official help says a useful skill states: when to use it, required inputs and access, the sequence of work, how to validate the result, what to return, and what requires approval.

Private skills are one library shared by all your Bots, though a Bot may need the relevant app or login to use one. To use a skill, ask the Bot for it by name.

When Teach a task is available, you can demonstrate a browser workflow instead of describing every step. Open a one-to-one Bot conversation and its computer view, choose Teach a task, describe the result, do the workflow once, stop the recording, and review the skill the Bot creates. Then test it on a safe example before you schedule it. Teaching records visible computer interaction for up to ten minutes. It does not record microphone audio. Avoid exposing secrets during the demonstration. The learned skill is a draft, so add decision rules, failure handling, and approval boundaries that may not be obvious from one example. Teach-by-demonstration may be enabled gradually. If the control isn’t visible, ask the Bot to create a skill from written instructions and the completed task.

  1. Do the job once in chat

    Run this week’s keto plan or a grocery list by hand, and fix the result until you’d happily reuse it.

  2. Save the method, not just the outcome

    Ask the Bot to save a skill with a clear name, the sources (calendar, meal rotation), the output (the same Page, a short reply), and the approval line: no Discord, no Giles email, no purchases.

  3. Name the stop conditions

    What should happen if the calendar can’t be read, or a store page has changed. Official help wants failure handling in the skill, not a silent guess.

  4. Test on a safe example

    Try the skill on a made-up week, or a store you don’t need to buy from. Keep sends behind your OK.

  5. Only then put it on a routine

    A skill is how. A routine is when. Don’t automate a method you haven’t watched once.

Copy and try 
Save the process we just used as a skill called "Weekly keto meal plan." When to use it: when I ask for next week's dinners, or when the Sunday planning routine runs. Sources: our usual dinner rotation and my iPhone calendar. Output: update the existing Meal Plan Page in place, then reply with only the link and two lines on what changed. Skip nights that already have plans. No health numbers. Never email Giles, post to Discord, or buy anything without my OK. If the calendar can't be read, say so and stop.

Tip of the week

If Teach a task isn’t visible, don’t wait. Ask the Bot to save a skill from the written steps and the completed task, then review the draft. Official help says availability may roll out gradually, and the written path is the fallback.

Use case spotlight: show the workflow, then refine the draft

On October 26, @kuma_baum pointed at the official guide: show Grok Bot a browser workflow, then refine the skill it creates. Record a demonstration, review the draft, and add failure handling before you schedule it. They also noted that availability is gradual, which matches the official page.

The next day, @BigAL1486 called Teach a task one of the least talked about and one of the best features, and said he uses it for a church calling that includes agendas, Google Docs and spreadsheets, emails, and pulling data from online reports. His post ended mid-sentence there, so that’s all I’ll take from it.

For you, a good first skill is one you already repeat: the weekly keto plan, or a grocery list from that plan.
